1. The Double-Header Row Trap: Row 1 contains research variable tags ('', 'X1', 'X2', ..., 'X23', 'Y'), while Row 2 holds the real column names (ID, LIMIT_BAL, ..., default payment next month)! Until Row 1 is deleted, every number is forced into Text!

4. Phase A — Step-by-Step in Microsoft Excel

Step A1 — Open .xls & Delete the Dummy X1..Y Row

  1. Open datasets/07-credit-clients/data/default of credit card clients.xls in Excel (sheet name: Data).
  2. Look at Row 1: B1 = X1, C1 = X2, …, Y1 = Y. Look at Row 2: A2 = ID, B2 = LIMIT_BAL, …, Y2 = default payment next month!
  3. Right-click row number 1 on the far left → click Delete. Now Row 1 has your clean headers and Rows 2–30,001 hold all 30,000 clients!
  4. Click View → Freeze Panes → Freeze Top Row.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Academic/research exports often carry a machine-readable variable-tag row (X1..X23, Y) ABOVE the human-readable header row. Any import engine — Excel, Power Query, a database — will promote whichever row it finds first. Leaving X1..Y in place forces every real number below into Text, because the columns' "first values" are strings like X2. One deleted row converts 25 text columns into 25 numeric ones.

Real-time example: Skip the delete and watch the cascade: Power BI loads 30,001 rows (the real header row became "data"), LIMIT_BAL can't be averaged (it's text), your default-rate measure errors or — worse — silently returns blank, and the CRO's first question becomes "why does your report say zero?" The 10-second right-click delete prevents an afternoon of type-migration archaeology. Notice also Freeze Top Row — with 25 columns and 30K rows, scrolling without headers means misreading PAY_4 as PAY_2, which in credit data is the difference between "last month late" and "4 months late".

What happens if you skip it: Mistake 1 in full: X-named columns, everything Text, garbage counts, and a dashboard that must be rebuilt from the import step anyway.

Smart-work tip: On ANY new file, read the first TWO rows before importing — header rows and unit rows hide above the data more often than beginners expect. If Power Query already promoted the wrong row, "Use First Row as Headers" twice (remove + promote) is the repair.

Step A2 — Add 3 Helper Columns (Columns Z, AA, AB)

  1. Column Z — EduLabel: =IF(D2=1,"1. Graduate School",IF(D2=2,"2. University",IF(D2=3,"3. High School","4. Other"))) You'll see: Z2 shows the text 2. University (row 2's EDUCATION = 2).
  2. Column AA — DelinquencyTier (from PAY_0 in G2): =IF(G2<=0,"1. Current / Revolving",IF(G2=1,"2. 1 Month Late","3. 2+ Months Late")) You'll see: AA2 shows 3. 2+ Months Late (row 2's PAY_0 = 2 — this client was already 2 months behind).
  3. Column AB — LimitBand (from LIMIT_BAL in B2): =IF(B2<=50000,"1. <=50K",IF(B2<=150000,"2. 51K-150K",IF(B2<=300000,"3. 151K-300K","4. >300K"))) You'll see: AB2 shows 1. <=50K — row 2's credit limit is 20000 (NT$, stored as a plain number).
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The CRO explicitly said: "Don't hand me cryptic numbers like Education=3 and PAY_0=2." These helper columns translate database codes into risk vocabulary. EduLabel also absorbs undocumented codes (0, 5, 6 → "4. Other", 468 clients) — real datasets contain codes the dictionary never mentioned, and if you don't bucket them, Power BI prints a bare "6" category on the chart. DelinquencyTier compresses PAY_0's −2…8 range into 3 actionable tiers, because collections teams act on "current / 1 month late / 2+ months late", not on 11 code values. LimitBand turns a continuous NT$ limit into 4 comparable exposure groups.

Real-time example: Why tiering matters so much here: inside PAY_0, a client 2 months late defaults at 69.55% and 3 months late at 75.78% — versus 13.83% for current clients. That's a cliff, not a slope. A chart of 11 raw code values buries the cliff; a 3-tier chart makes the red wall visible from across the room — and tells the collections team exactly where to send the dialers.

What happens if you skip it: Charts show "EDUCATION = 6" categories, slicers list −2 to 8 as separate items, and the dashboard violates the client's very first instruction.

Smart-work tip: Always click a coded column's filter dropdown and compare its values against the data dictionary — 0, 5, 6 hiding in EDUCATION is how you find undocumented codes BEFORE the meeting. Then fold them into an honest "Other" bucket instead of pretending they don't exist.

Step A3 — PivotTable Check

Insert a PivotTable: Rows = DelinquencyTier, Columns = LimitBand, Values = Average of default payment next month (formatted as 0.0%). Confirm overall Grand Total = 22.1% (22.12%), 3. 2+ Months Late = 69.6%, and Save As 07-credit-clients-clean.xlsx (saving as modern .xlsx is also much faster for Power BI than legacy 97–2003 .xls!).
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Because default payment next month is already a 0/1 flag, Average of the flag IS the default rate — the pivot computes 6,636 ÷ 30,000 = 22.12% with no formula at all. The Tier × Band cross-tab previews the dashboard's most important interaction: does late-payment risk compound with low credit limit? And the .xlsx save matters for speed: Power BI's .xls connector is legacy and slower.

Real-time example: Read the cross-tab the way a credit committee would: the "2+ Months Late × ≤50K" cell is the portfolio's furnace — severely delinquent borrowers with tiny limits, where default rates spike toward 70%+. Low limits aren't just an exposure size question; they're a who got approved question, and the cross-tab shows the two risk factors stacking. That cell is precisely what Visual 4's red traffic-light icon will flag for the collections team — you've now seen the story in Excel before designing it in Power BI.

What happens if you skip it: You lose the compounding insight (tier and band interact), and the dashboard gets built around two separate bar charts that answer less than the cross-tab did in five minutes.

Smart-work tip: 0/1 flags make pivots into instant rate calculators — remember this property (Average of flag = rate) for churn, fraud, late-pay, or any binary outcome. It replaces COUNTIF chains everywhere.

5. Phase B — Complete Power BI & Charts Journey

Stage 1 & 2 — Clean Headers & Risk Tiers in Power Query

  1. Connect to your saved 07-credit-clients-clean.xlsx (or raw .xls) → tick Data → click Transform Data. Rename query to Clients.
  2. If connecting to the raw .xls: Power Query promoted X1..Y as headers, leaving ID, LIMIT_BAL... in Row 1! Simply click Home → Use First Row as Headers one more time, then select all 25 columns (Ctrl+A) and click Transform → Detect Data Type! Verify all 25 columns are 123 (Whole Number).
  3. Rename column default payment next month to DefaultFlag (much cleaner to type in DAX!).
  4. Add Conditional Columns (if not already in your Excel file):
    • EducationLabel: 1 → 1. Graduate School · 2 → 2. University · 3 → 3. High School · Else → 4. Other.
    • DelinquencyTier: PAY_0 <= 0 → 1. Current / Revolving · PAY_0 = 1 → 2. 1 Month Late · Else → 3. 2+ Months Late.
    • LimitBand: LIMIT_BAL <= 50000 → 1. <=50K · <= 150000 → 2. 51K-150K · <= 300000 → 3. 151K-300K · Else → 4. >300K.
    • GenderLabel: SEX = 1 → Male · Else → Female.
  5. Click Home → Close & Apply.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Power Query is where the repair becomes repeatable: "Use First Row as Headers" + "Detect Data Type" recorded as steps means next quarter's .xls export cleans itself. Renaming default payment next month → DefaultFlag is developer hygiene — DAX like SUM(Clients[DefaultFlag]) reads cleanly and never breaks over a 25-character name with spaces. The conditional label columns mirror your Excel helpers, but now as refresh-proof query steps.

Real-time example: Note the deliberate choice to KEEP raw coded columns (SEX, EDUCATION, PAY_0) alongside their labels: PAY_0 stays numeric because measures like Severe Delinquent Clients = CALCULATE(..., PAY_0 >= 2) need the real number for comparisons — a text label "3. 2+ Months Late" can't be compared with >=. This is a pattern you'll use everywhere: keep the numeric key for math, add the label for display, hide whichever the report shouldn't touch.

What happens if you skip it: Every manual fix lives only in this one PBIX; the DAX gets written against fragile long column names; and label-only tables lose the ability to do numeric tier logic.

Smart-work tip: Name flags after what 1 means (DefaultFlag), not what the column was called in the research paper. Future readers — including you — should understand measures without opening the data dictionary.

Stage 3 & 4 — Model Hygiene & Credit-Risk DAX Measures

Set ID, SEX, EDUCATION, MARRIAGE, and PAY_0..PAY_6 Summarization = Don't summarize (or hide raw code columns!). Then create these measures:
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Power BI's default summarization is SUM — which is correct for money and counts but nonsensical for identifiers and codes. Left at default, dragging ID or PAY_0 onto any value field produces instant garbage, and those garbage choices sit at the top of every field list tempting beginners. Setting Don't summarize forces the model to refuse implicit aggregation; hiding the raw codes (while keeping them for DAX) keeps the Report view clean without losing the numeric keys the measures need for comparisons like PAY_0 >= 2.

Real-time example: See the failure it prevents: PAY_0 holds repayment-delay codes from −2 to 8 for 30,000 clients. Summed by default, a card cheerfully reports something like "Sum of PAY_0 ≈ 90,000" — a number with no meaning a human can state, yet it looks official on a dashboard. And SEX (coded 1/2) summed gives a fake "headcount". With Don't summarize, Power BI blocks these implicit aggregations and the analyst must write an explicit, reviewed measure instead.

What happens if you skip it: "Sum of ID"-style junk cards ship in decks, field lists mislead every future developer, and implicit totals silently disagree with the real DAX measures sitting next to them.

Smart-work tip: Hygiene sweep on every model: identifiers and coded columns get Don't summarize or Hidden; only genuinely additive columns (money, counts) keep default aggregation. It's the Set 04 ZIP-code rule generalized: identifiers are labels, not quantities.

Total Clients = COUNTROWS ( 'Clients' )
// Check: 30,000

Defaulted Clients = SUM ( 'Clients'[DefaultFlag] )
// Check: 6,636

Default Rate % = DIVIDE ( [Defaulted Clients], [Total Clients], 0 )
// Format: 0.00% -> Check: 22.12%

Risk Ceiling % = 0.20
// 20.0% CRO risk threshold

Avg Credit Limit = AVERAGE ( 'Clients'[LIMIT_BAL] )
// Format: $#,0 -> Check: $167,484 (NT$)

Credit Utilization % =
DIVIDE ( SUM ( 'Clients'[BILL_AMT1] ), SUM ( 'Clients'[LIMIT_BAL] ), 0 )
// Format: 0.0% -> Check: 30.6% (30.58%)

Severe Delinquent Clients = CALCULATE ( [Total Clients], 'Clients'[PAY_0] >= 2 )
// Check: 3,130 high-risk accounts (69.55% default rate!)
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Two subtleties make these measures "credit-grade" rather than "spreadsheet-grade". (1) Credit Utilization % divides one month's statement balance by the limit — a portfolio-level ratio computed as sum÷sum, NOT a sum of six monthly snapshots. Summing BILL_AMT1..6 would multiply every revolving balance by 6 and report "utilization" over 180% — mathematically legal, financially absurd. (2) Risk Ceiling % = 0.20 is a measure, not a typed number, so the Gauge and every conditional-formatting rule reference one editable threshold.

Real-time example: Picture the CRO validating your card: Total Clients 30,000, Defaults 6,636, rate 22.12% — breaching the 20% ceiling. Now the useful part: Severe Delinquent Clients isolates 3,130 accounts (10.4% of the book) carrying a 69.55% default rate. That tiny segment produces 2,177 of the 6,636 defaults — a third of all losses from a tenth of the accounts. That one measure reframes the whole meeting from "the portfolio is bad" to "3,130 accounts are the problem — here is the list".

What happens if you skip it: Utilization gets computed from summed bills (nonsense), the 20% ceiling gets hardcoded in three places and drifts out of sync, and the severity of the delinquent segment stays buried inside the blended 22.12%.

Smart-work tip: Before summing any column family like BILL_AMT1..6, ask whether they are sequential snapshots of the same thing (balances, inventory, headcount) — if yes, pick ONE snapshot or average them; never add. And define risk thresholds as measures the moment a client mentions a number like "20% ceiling".

Reason: The client's exact words: "an interactive root-cause tree so my collections managers can drill." A Decomposition Tree is the visual that makes reports self-serve: instead of the analyst pre-deciding the 10 charts the CRO might want, the tree holds one measure and four candidate splits, and the AI "High value" logic proposes the most informative cut at each level, live. Real-time example: In the meeting: root reads 22.12% (breach!). Visual 3 Credit Limit Exposure vs. Default Rate — Combo Chart

Put LimitBand on X-axis, [Total Clients] on Column y-axis, and [Default Rate %] on Line y-axis. Shows default risk dropping smoothly from 31.8% (<=50K) → 23.7% → 16.8% → 13.3% (>300K).

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Risk management always asks two questions at once: how many accounts (exposure = columns) and how likely to default (probability = line). Plotting only the rate hides where the volume sits; plotting only counts hides where the danger is. The combo shows both scales against one ordered X-axis — and the smooth downward slope of the line is itself a finding: credit limits are an effective risk screen.

Real-time example: Read the story: the two biggest columns sit on the left — 7,676 accounts in ≤50K at 31.79% and 8,724 in 51–150K at 23.72% — meaning most of the book lives in the highest-risk bands. The rate falls to 13.26% above NT$300K. That gap (31.8% vs 13.3%) is the empirical justification for the CRO's underwriting policy: the limit a customer is granted correlates directly with how much risk they carry, and the chart quantifies exactly how much. If a future committee proposes relaxing low-limit approvals, this visual is the counter-evidence.

What happens if you skip it: The exposure-vs-risk trade-off stays abstract; the meeting debates "low limits are risky" as opinion instead of reading a measured 18-point spread across ordered bands.

Smart-work tip: Keep the bands in business order (1→4, thanks to the numbered labels) — an unordered X-axis would scramble the monotonic slope that makes the insight obvious. This combo pattern (counts + rate over an ordered dimension) is the standard for any portfolio/segment risk review.

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: A collections team doesn't consume analysis — it consumes a work queue. The Tier × Band matrix with traffic-light icons converts the model into their daily triage tool: red = call today, amber = monitor, green = leave alone. Icons beat background shading here because they encode action categories (3 discrete states), not magnitude — and they survive printing and color-blind viewing better than pure color gradients.

Real-time example: Scan the finished matrix as a collections manager: the red row is "2+ Months Late" across every band (69.55% average), and the reddest corner is severe-delinquent ≤50K accounts. Meanwhile "Current × >300K" sits green at ~9.3% — no dialer time wasted there. The thresholds (0.18 / 0.30) are anchored to the CRO's 20% risk appetite, so a cell's icon literally means "this segment is above/below our stated tolerance" — the visual speaks the same policy language the risk committee does.

What happens if you skip it: The team receives rates as raw decimals and triages by gut feel; or worse, everyone optimizes for the loudest average (22.12%) instead of the highest-priority cells.

Smart-work tip: Tie icon thresholds to official policy numbers (the 20% ceiling), not arbitrary round values — then the dashboard enforces policy automatically after every refresh. Keep account counts next to the icon so effort is allocated by cell size AND severity.
