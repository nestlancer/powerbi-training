• 1,046 stores are traditional Wal-Mart discount stores (conversion = blank, date_super = blank).
• 1,557 stores opened as Wal-Mart and later converted to a Supercenter (conversion = 1).

4. Phase A — Step-by-Step in Microsoft Excel

Step A1 — Import CSV & Protect ZIP Codes

  1. Open Excel → Data → From Text/CSV → select datasets/04-walmart-openings/data/walmart-openings.csv → click Transform Data (so Excel doesn't misread OPENDATE before you see it!).
  2. Set ZIPCODE to Text, YEAR/MONTH/DAY to Whole Number, and click Close & Load.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Two identity columns are under attack the moment this CSV touches a spreadsheet engine. ZIP codes look numeric but are labels — East Coast ZIPs like 07501 (New Jersey) lose their leading zero and become 7501, breaking every join or lookup by ZIP. And OPENDATE's 2-digit years are a loaded trap: Excel's cutoff rules will decide — for you, silently — whether 62 means 1962 or 2062. Going through Transform Data first means you see and type these columns yourself instead of letting the import guess.

Real-time example: Imagine the retail VP asks: "How many stores opened per year in the 1960s?" If Excel "helpfully" read 7/1/62 as July 1, 2062, your time charts now contain stores that opened 36 years in the future — and the 1960s count comes out near zero. The analysis is wrong before a single chart was drawn, and because the error is plausible-looking, it survives reviews.

What happens if you skip it: Leading-zero ZIPs break, 2-digit years scatter stores across two centuries, and nobody notices until a map or a timeline looks "weird" — usually in front of the client.

Smart-work tip: Treat any identifier (ZIP, phone, store number, FIPS code) as Text on sight — identifiers are names that happen to use digits. And never let a date column pass through an import un-inspected when it has 2-digit years.

Step A2 — Build 3 Bulletproof Helper Columns (Columns Q, R, S)

  1. Column Q — OpenDateClean (True 4-Digit Date): Instead of trusting 2-digit OPENDATE, combine YEAR (P2), MONTH (N2), and DAY (O2)! In cell Q1 type OpenDateClean, and in Q2 type:
    =DATE(P2, N2, O2)
    Format column Q as yyyy-mm-dd. Check row 2: 1962-07-01 (never 2062!).
  2. Column R — StoreHistory (3-Way Lifecycle Label): In cell R1 type StoreHistory, and in R2 type:
    =IF(D2=1, "Converted to Supercenter", IF(D2=0, "Opened as Supercenter", "Discount Wal-Mart"))
    Watch carefully: Make sure you test ISBLANK(D2) first if Excel treats blank as 0! Even safer formula:
    =IF(ISBLANK(D2), "Discount Wal-Mart", IF(D2=1, "Converted to Supercenter", "Opened as Supercenter")) ✅ (Always use ISBLANK in Excel because Excel evaluates "" = 0 as TRUE if you aren't careful!)
  3. Column S — Decade: In cell S1 type Decade, and in S2 type:
    =INT(P2/10)*10 & "s" (turns 1962 into 1960s, 1990 into 1990s).
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Each helper column embodies one professional principle. OpenDateClean = DATE(P2,N2,O2): when a file offers BOTH an ambiguous text date (7/1/62) AND the exact components (YEAR=1962), reconstruct from the authoritative parts instead of parsing the ambiguous text. StoreHistory with ISBLANK(): blank ("never converted") and 0 ("built as Supercenter from day one") are two different business states that a careless =IF(D2=0,...) would merge into one. Decade: time trends become readable when years are grouped into eras.

Real-time example: Why the ISBLANK order matters: Excel compares blank cells loosely, so IF(D2=0,...) fires on BOTH the 1,046 never-converted stores AND the 389 built-as-Supercenter stores — merging 1,435 distinct rows into one category. Your 3-slice donut becomes a 2-slice donut and the whole "conversion vs built-new" analysis the VP asked for quietly disappears. One function call protects 1,046 rows of truth.

What happens if you skip it: Dates span 1962–2062 (future stores!), the lifecycle story collapses, and decade-level charts can't exist.

Smart-work tip: When a dataset ships redundant "raw + parsed" columns, ask "which one is authoritative?" and build from that one. Here P/N/O came straight from the source system, so DATE(P2,N2,O2) is 100% bulletproof — no locale, no century cutoffs, no guessing.

Step A3 — PivotTable Verification

  1. Insert a PivotTable on a new sheet: Rows = STRSTATE, Columns = StoreHistory, Values = Count of storenum.
  2. Sort Grand Total Largest to Smallest: confirm TX = 315 (200 Converted, 53 Opened as Supercenter, 62 Discount Wal-Mart), FL = 175, CA = 159 (only 12 Converted!), and Grand Total = 2,992!
  3. Save as 04-walmart-openings-clean.xlsx.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: This is a cross-tab pivot (state × lifecycle), not a flat list — and cross-tabs exist to expose differences in composition, which a totals-only pivot hides. Reading Rows × Columns together answers the client's exact question: which states converted existing stores into Supercenters, and which barely converted at all?

Real-time example: The pivot instantly surfaces the dataset's best story: Texas has 315 stores with 200 conversions, while California — the #3 state with 159 stores — converted only 12 (7.5%!). That asymmetry is the jumping-off point for the real-estate insight (California municipalities passed anti-supercenter zoning ordinances). If you'd only pivoted "total stores by state", TX 315 and CA 159 would look like pure size ranking, and the zoning story — the actual reason this data is interesting — would never appear.

What happens if you skip it: You verify totals (good) but miss the structure (better), and your dashboard reproduces numbers nobody asked questions about.

Smart-work tip: Whenever two categorical dimensions might interact ("does conversion differ by state?"), build a cross-tab pivot in Excel BEFORE designing Power BI visuals — it's a 3-minute prototype of your dashboard's main chart, and it tells you whether the story exists at all.

5. Phase B — Complete Power BI & Charts Journey

Stage 1 & 2 — Clean Dates & Lifecycles in Power Query

  1. Click Home → Text/CSV → select data/walmart-openings.csv → Transform Data. Rename query to Stores.
  2. Build Clean Opening Date from YEAR, MONTH, DAY: Click Add Column → Custom Column → Name: OpenDate → Formula:
    #date([YEAR], [MONTH], [DAY]) → click OK, and set its type icon to Date!
  3. Build StoreHistory Conditional Column: Click Add Column → Conditional Column → Name: StoreHistory:
    • If conversion equals null → Output Discount Wal-Mart
    • Else if conversion equals 1 → Output Converted to Supercenter
    • Else → Output Opened as Supercenter → click OK.
  4. Build LocationState for Unambiguous US Mapping: Click Add Column → Custom Column → Name: LocationState → Formula: [STRSTATE] & ", USA" (prevents GA from mapping to the country Georgia in Eastern Europe!).
  5. Ensure LAT and LON are 1.2 (Decimal Number), ZIPCODE is ABC (Text), and conversion is 123 (Whole Number). Click Home → Close & Apply.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Three Power Query moves replace the Excel helper columns with refreshable ones: #date([YEAR],[MONTH],[DAY]) rebuilds the bulletproof date, the Conditional Column's null branch keeps blank ≠ 0 ≠ 1 separate (protecting the 1,046 never-converted stores), and LocationState = [STRSTATE] & ", USA" de-duplicates geography at the source. Everything is now a recorded step that replays on refresh.

Real-time example: The ", USA" suffix looks trivial until you see the failure it prevents: Power BI geocodes "GA" — and the filled map lights up the country Georgia in Eastern Europe, so your most interesting state (68.4% conversion rate!) vanishes from America. Same trap: "WA" (state vs Washington, D.C. vs Western Australia). Ambiguous short codes are a geocoder's nightmare; appending the country and setting Data Category = State or Province removes the guesswork permanently.

What happens if you skip it: Pins land on the wrong continent, the 2-digit year bug resurrects, blanks get mislabeled — and every one of those bugs re-appears after every refresh because the fix was never recorded.

Smart-work tip: Fix geography in Power Query, not in the visual: a measure or label suffix fixed on one chart doesn't help the other four. Source-level cleaning is written once and protects every map, slicer, and export downstream.

Stage 3 — Model View: Geo Categories & Calendar Table

  1. Set Geo Categories on Stores (Column tools → Data category):
    • LAT → Latitude (and Summarization = Don't summarize!)
    • LON → Longitude (and Summarization = Don't summarize!)
    • LocationState → State or Province; ZIPCODE → Postal code.
  2. Create a Date Table in Data view → New table: 
    Calendar =
ADDCOLUMNS (
    CALENDAR ( DATE ( 1962, 1, 1 ), DATE ( 2006, 12, 31 ) ),
    "Year", YEAR ( [Date] ),
    "Decade", INT ( YEAR ( [Date] ) / 10 ) * 10 & "s"
)
    Mark as date table and relate Calendar[Date] (1) → Stores[OpenDate] (*).
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: LAT/LON get three pieces of metadata, and each prevents one specific failure: Data category = Latitude/Longitude tells Power BI "these are coordinates, not statistics"; Don't summarize stops the visual from averaging 2,992 pins into ONE pin; and the relationship from a 1962–2006 Calendar gives the running-total measure a continuous axis to walk (including years with zero openings).

Real-time example: Here's the famous failure this prevents: Power BI defaults LAT to Average of LAT ≈ 37.8 and LON to Average of LON ≈ −90.6 — and the map shows a single glowing pin in the middle of Kansas, as if Walmart built one store for the entire nation. It is the single most common "my map is broken!" support question in Power BI training, and the fix is 10 seconds of metadata. The Decade column in the Calendar, meanwhile, is what lets a slicer animate 44 years of expansion in 4 button clicks.

What happens if you skip it: One pin in Kansas, charts that can't filter by decade, and a cumulative measure that skips empty years and draws a jagged, misleading curve.

Smart-work tip: Coordinates are positions, never quantities — any default aggregation on them is a bug in waiting. Check Summarization = Don't summarize on every geo column the day you import, and build the Calendar table wide enough to cover every date in the model (1962, not 2013 — each project gets its own range).

Stage 4 — DAX Measures (Including Your First Cumulative Running Total!)

Total Stores = COUNTROWS ( 'Stores' )
// Check: 2,992

Supercenters = CALCULATE ( [Total Stores], 'Stores'[type_store] = "Supercenter" )
// Check: 1,946

Converted Stores = SUM ( 'Stores'[conversion] )
// Check: 1,557 (SUM safely ignores nulls and 0s, counting only conversion = 1!)

Conversion Rate % = DIVIDE ( [Converted Stores], [Total Stores], 0 )
// Format: 0.0% -> Check: 52.0% overall (TX = 63.5%, CA = 7.5%)

Supercenter Share % = DIVIDE ( [Supercenters], [Total Stores], 0 )
// Format: 0.0% -> Check: 65.0%

Cumulative Stores =
CALCULATE (
    [Total Stores],
    FILTER (
        ALL ( 'Calendar' ),
        'Calendar'[Date] <= MAX ( 'Calendar'[Date] )
    )
)
// ADVANCED RUNNING TOTAL! Starts at 1 in 1962 and climbs smoothly to 2,992 in 2006!
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Each measure encodes a reusable DAX pattern. Converted Stores = SUM(conversion) is an elegant null-safe count: 1s contribute 1, 0s and nulls contribute nothing — so a SUM becomes a conditional count with zero extra logic. Cumulative Stores is your first running total: ALL('Calendar') temporarily removes the date filter, then 'Calendar'[Date] <= MAX('Calendar'[Date]) re-adds only the dates up to "now" — summing every store opened so far.

Real-time example: Why SUM works where COUNT fails: COUNT(conversion) would count every non-blank row (1,557 + 389 = 1,946 — wrong!), but SUM reads the column as 1/0/null flags, landing exactly on 1,557. And the running total tells the growth story a yearly bar never can: the fleet crossed 1,000 stores around 1988 and doubled again within 10 years — an S-curve of compounding expansion the VP can annotate ("when did we reach half today's fleet?").

What happens if you skip it: Without the ALL pattern, "cumulative" at each year equals just that year's openings (the filter never lifts), and the line collapses to a copy of the columns — the #1 beginner running-total bug. Without null-aware counting, conversion counts silently include stores that never converted.

Smart-work tip: Memorize the skeleton CALCULATE([X], FILTER(ALL(DateTable), Date <= MAX(Date))) — it's the universal YTD, lifetime-to-date, and inventory-accumulation pattern. Verify the endpoints (1 in 1962, 2,992 in 2006) and one midpoint; three checks prove the whole curve.

Reason: A choropleth (filled map) encodes a rate as color intensity — the fastest possible read for "which regions differ?" Conditional formatting binds the shade to [Conversion Rate %], so the map updates automatically when any slicer changes. Real-time example: The gradient instantly separates two Americas: the Deep South (GA 68.4%, TX 63.5%, MO 61.5%) glows green — Walmart's historical home turf where big-box Supercenter conversions met little resistance — while California stays pale yellow at 7.5%, reflecting municipal anti-supercenter zoning ordinances. What happens if you skip it: The same rates in a table (52.0%, 63.5%, 7.5%…) take deliberate reading to compare across 41 states; on a map, the regional pattern jumps out pre-attentively — the audience "gets it" before you finish the sentence.

Smart-work tip: Always pin the gradient's min/max explicitly (0% → 70%) instead of auto: auto-scaling makes every map look "colorful" even when rates barely differ, exaggerating meaningless differences. Fixed scales keep multiple maps (or time periods) comparable and honest.

Columns show annual building waves peaking in 1990 (178 stores) and 1991 (161 stores), while the secondary-axis S-curve line climbs steadily to 2,992!

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: This combo pairs two different mathematical questions about the same timeline: pace (columns: how many NEW stores per year?) and accumulated scale (line: how big is the fleet so far?). They need separate axes because one is a small yearly number (max 178) and the other is a growing total (up to 2,992) — on one axis, either the peaks or the curve would be crushed.

Real-time example: Read it the way an executive does: the columns reveal when Walmart pushed hardest (peaks of 178 in 1990 and 161 in 1991 — the Supercenter gold-rush years) and when it cooled; the S-curve line reveals what those years added up to — half the fleet arriving after the mid-1980s. Together they answer both "when was the push?" and "how did we end up at 2,992?" in one glance. A speedometer and an odometer, on one chart.

What happens if you skip it: Two separate charts force mental chart-matching; one chart with both fields on a single axis renders the 178-store columns invisible next to a 2,992 line — the flat-column trap from Set 01, in a different costume.

Smart-work tip: Whenever you have "events per period" + "cumulative total", this combo is the standard shape (works identically for sales + revenue-to-date, hires + headcount, tickets + backlog). Your ALL('Calendar') running-total measure is what makes the line true at every year.

Visual 5 & 6 Lifecycle Donut Chart + Top 12 States Stacked Bar

1. Add a Donut chart with StoreHistory in Legend and [Total Stores] in Values (3 crisp slices — ideal use of a Donut chart!).
2. Add a Stacked bar chart with STRSTATE on Y-axis (filtered to Top 12 by [Total Stores]), [Total Stores] on X-axis, and StoreHistory in Legend.

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The Donut gets exactly the situation donuts are FOR: 3 mutually-exclusive parts of one whole (52.0% converted / 35.0% discount / 13.0% built-as-Supercenter). With more than ~4 slices, pie/donut charts become angle-guessing games — but 3 clean slices read instantly. The Top-12 Stacked bar then breaks that same fleet mix down by state, because composition differences between states are the real story, not the national average.

Real-time example: Look down the stacked bars and the state story writes itself: Georgia's segment is almost all Supercenter (68.4% conversion), Texas is dominated by conversions, and California's bar is conspicuously mostly plain discount-store gray — the zoning-restriction footprint from Visual 3, now visible as bar composition. National averages (52.0%) hide this; per-state stacks reveal which markets Walmart could re-format and which it had to leave alone.

What happens if you skip it: A stakeholder sees "52% converted nationally" and assumes it's true everywhere; the CA exception stays buried in a table. And an all-states bar chart (41 bars) drowns the Top 12 that matter.

Smart-work tip: Donut for parts-of-one-whole with ≤4 slices; stacked bar for comparing composition ACROSS categories. Together: the national mix on top, the state-by-state mix underneath — one page answers "what's the mix?" and "where does it differ?".
