4. Phase A — Step-by-Step in Microsoft Excel

Step A1 — Import the CSV Properly (Don't Just Double-Click!)

  1. Open a blank Excel workbook → click the Data tab on the top ribbon → click From Text/CSV (far left).
  2. Select datasets/02-college-majors/data/recent-grads.csv → click Import.
  3. In the preview window, confirm Delimiter = Comma and check that 21 columns appear cleanly → click Load. (Excel automatically loads it as a formatted Excel Table named recent_grads — the hyphen becomes an underscore because Excel Table names forbid hyphens — and as you scroll inside the table its header names replace the A, B, C column letters, so headers stay visible even though Freeze Panes is never turned on!)
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Double-clicking a CSV opens it in Excel's "quick and dirty" mode: Excel guesses every column's type and often mangles data silently. Importing through Data → From Text/CSV shows you a preview first, lets you confirm the delimiter, and creates a proper Excel Table with a reusable connection — the data arrives the way the file actually stored it.

Real-time example: Classic CSV disasters from double-clicking: a ZIP-style code 0113 becomes the number 113, a long ID turns into scientific notation 1.13E+12, and free-text like NA or MEN_ONLY can get re-typed into dates or formulas. On a dataset like recent-grads.csv — 173 rows × 21 columns of headcounts, rates, and salaries — one silent type mistake corrupts every downstream average before you even start.

What happens if you skip it: You inherit hidden type errors you won't notice until a PivotTable looks strange, and because double-clicking creates no refreshable connection, next year's updated CSV means starting the whole import by hand again.

Smart-work tip: In the preview window, actually read the first rows of every column — 60 seconds of inspection beats 60 minutes of debugging. The green Table + frozen headers that Excel gives you afterward are free gifts: use them.

Step A2 — Inspect Row 23 (Food Science) & Format Numbers

  1. Scroll down to Row 23 (Rank = 22, FOOD SCIENCE). Notice cells D23:F23 (Total, Men, Women) and H23 (ShareWomen) are empty! Do NOT delete this row — its Median ($53,000) and Employed (3,149) are valid and needed for the 173-row pay check!
  2. Format columns H (ShareWomen) and O (Unemployment_rate) as Percentage (1 decimal).
  3. Format columns P:R (Median, P25th, P75th) as Currency ($#,##0, 0 decimals).
  4. Format headcount columns D:F, J:N, and S:U as Number with comma separator (#,##0).
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: This step teaches the most important data-cleaning distinction in analytics: missing ≠ zero. Row 23 (Food Science) has 4 blank cells because the census simply did not publish its gender split — the information is unknown, not zero women. Deleting the row would throw away its perfectly valid salary ($53,000) and employment (3,149 employed) data; replacing blanks with 0 would invent a fact that was never measured.

Real-time example: Suppose you "fixed" ShareWomen blank by typing 0. Food Science instantly looks like a 100%-male major in every chart — a fabricated statistic presented to a Dean. Conversely, if you deleted the row, your majors count drops to 172 and every "of 173 majors" statement in your report becomes wrong. Both mistakes are invisible until someone who knows the data checks you.

What happens if you skip it: Unformatted decimals like 0.0789 read as noise, so humans verify nothing; and there is no record anywhere that row 23's blanks were a deliberate decision rather than an oversight.

Smart-work tip: When you find blanks, run through the 3-question checklist: Is it truly unknown? Does the rest of the row stay valid? Should anyone downstream see a fake 0? Keeping the row with nulls — like Power BI does automatically — is the professional answer here.

Step A3 — Add 3 Helper Columns (Columns V, W, X)

  1. Column V — MajorClean (Fix ALL CAPS with PROPER): In cell V1 type MajorClean. In cell V2 type:
    =PROPER(C2)
    Watch PETROLEUM ENGINEERING turn into clean Petroleum Engineering across all 173 rows! You'll see: V2 shows Petroleum Engineering instantly — text results display exactly as returned, no number format involved.
  2. Column W — PayBand (Salary Histogram Buckets): In cell W1 type PayBand. In cell W2 type:
    =IF(P2<30000,"1. Under $30K",IF(P2<40000,"2. $30K–$39K",IF(P2<50000,"3. $40K–$49K",IF(P2<70000,"4. $50K–$69K","5. $70K+"))))
    Why number prefixes 1–5? So any PivotTable or beginner chart automatically sorts the salary buckets from lowest to highest! You'll see: W2 shows the text 5. $70K+ (row 2's median, $110,000, lands in the top band) — the $ inside is part of the label text, not cell formatting.
  3. Column X — PaySpread (75th minus 25th Percentile Upside): In cell X1 type PaySpread. In cell X2 type:
    =R2-Q2
    This measures earning upside within each major (e.g., $125,000 - $95,000 = $30,000 for Petroleum Engineering). You'll see: X2 shows plain 30000 — General format from a CSV import, with no $ and no thousands comma (the $30,000 in this sentence is prose, not the cell display). Optional polish: format X (or P:R) as Currency $#,##0 and it reads $30,000.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Each helper column answers a different real question. MajorClean fixes presentation (ALL CAPS reads like shouting and wraps badly on chart axes). PayBand turns 173 different salaries into 5 comparable buckets for a histogram. PaySpread measures upside — how much a 75th-percentile graduate out-earns a 25th-percentile one — which a raw median alone can never show.

Real-time example: A student asks the advising office: "Is Petroleum Engineering really that much better, or just at the top?" Median says $110,000; PaySpread adds the nuance: even within Petroleum, the spread is $30,000 — so the honest answer is "high floor AND high ceiling". The numbered prefixes in PayBand ("1. Under $30K", "2. $30K–$39K"...) are a sneaky-smart trick: text sorts alphabetically, so building the number INTO the label means every future PivotTable and chart sorts the salary ladder correctly with zero extra effort.

What happens if you skip it: Slicers display PETROLEUM ENGINEERING in full caps, the histogram's bars land in alphabetical order ($70K+ first!), and no one can answer the "how much upside?" question without manual math per major.

Smart-work tip: Encode sort order into labels when you can't add a separate sort column — it's a legitimate pro shortcut for Excel-only workflows. One formula per question, filled down once by the Table, reused forever.

Step A4 — Validate with an Excel PivotTable

  1. Click inside the table → Insert → PivotTable → New Worksheet → OK. Rename the sheet Pivot_Check.
  2. Drag Major_category to Rows.
  3. Drag Major to Values (automatically becomes Count of Major — confirm Grand Total = 173).
  4. Drag Median to Values → click it in the Values box → Value Field Settings → Summarize value field by: Average → Number Format: Currency (0 decimals) → OK.
  5. Drag Total to Values (Sum of Total = 6,771,654).
  6. Sort by Average of Median Largest to Smallest: confirm Engineering = $57,383 at the top, Psychology & Social Work = $30,100 at the bottom, and Grand Total Average = $40,151!
  7. Save the workbook as 02-college-majors-clean.xlsx.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The pivot verifies three things at once: completeness (Count of Major = 173 proves no row was lost or duplicated in import), correct summarization (Median must be AVERAGED — summing 173 salaries produces a nonsense "$6.95M total median"), and your target numbers (Engineering #1 at $57,383, grand average $40,151) that the Power BI dashboard must reproduce exactly.

Real-time example: When your Power BI card later shows $40,151, you already know it's right — the pivot said so. When the Dean asks "which field should we market hardest?", you can say "Engineering: highest median pay $57,383 across 29 majors" with full confidence, because two independent tools agree. That is the habit that separates analysts from spreadsheet-fillers.

What happens if you skip it: Power BI defaults numeric columns to SUM; if nobody verified the pivot first, the very first draft dashboard may report "Total Median = $6,946,200" and the error travels into every percent-of-total calculation built on top of it.

Smart-work tip: On ANY new import, validate in this order: row count → column count → one spot-checked total. Count first (fastest to debug), values second. Save the clean copy under the project-named convention so it's findable months later.

5. Phase B — Complete Power BI & Charts Journey

Stage 1 & 2 — Connect to Raw CSV & Clean in Power Query

  1. In Power BI Desktop, click Home → Text/CSV → select data/recent-grads.csv → click Transform Data.
  2. Rename the query on the right from recent-grads to Majors.
  3. Convert ALL CAPS to Title Case in 2 clicks: Right-click the Major column header → Transform → Capitalize Each Word!
  4. Check Data Types:
    • Rank, Major_code, Total, Men, Women, Sample_size, Employed, Full_time, Part_time, Full_time_year_round, Unemployed, College_jobs, Non_college_jobs, Low_wage_jobs → 123 (Whole Number). Notice row 22 (Food Science) shows null in Total/Men/Women/ShareWomen — Power BI handles null cleanly without counting it as zero!
    • ShareWomen, Unemployment_rate → 1.2 (Decimal Number).
    • Median, P25th, P75th → $ (Fixed decimal number) or 123 (Whole Number).
  5. Add the Salary Band Column: Click Add Column → Conditional Column → Name: PayBand:
    • If Median is less than 30000 → 1. Under $30K
    • Else if Median is less than 40000 → 2. $30K–$39K
    • Else if Median is less than 50000 → 3. $40K–$49K
    • Else if Median is less than 70000 → 4. $50K–$69K
    • Else → 5. $70K+ → click OK, set type to ABC (Text).
  6. Click Home → Close & Apply.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Power Query is where cleaning becomes repeatable. "Capitalize Each Word" replaces your PROPER() formula; data-type clicks replace formatting; the Conditional Column rebuilds PayBand — and every action is saved as an Applied Step that replays on next year's updated CSV with one Refresh click.

Real-time example: Watch how Power Query treats Food Science's blanks: it shows them as null and keeps them null — it does NOT quietly turn them into 0 like a careless hand-cleanup would. That means your later Women Share % measure stays honest (Food Science simply drops out of gender math) without you writing a single defensive formula. This is the engine protecting you from the exact trap in Step A2.

What happens if you skip it: Manually fixing the CSV in Excel means every new academic year starts with re-doing the same cleanup, and one forgotten step (say, capitalization) leaves this year's slicers full of COMPUTER SCIENCE while last year's notes say Computer Science.

Smart-work tip: Rename the query to Majors — DAX then reads like a sentence: COUNTROWS('Majors'). Always do type fixes in Power Query rather than in report-view later, because types fixed upstream fix EVERYWHERE (charts, slicers, filters, exports).

  1. This dataset is a single 173-row cross-sectional benchmark table — no Date Table is needed because there are no dates.
  2. In the Data pane, click Rank → on the Column tools ribbon change Summarization → Don't summarize. Repeat for Major_code. (This prevents Power BI from ever summing Major Codes!)
  3. Hide raw rate columns ShareWomen and Unemployment_rate (right-click → Hide) so report users use your accurate weighted DAX measures instead!
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Two silent traps get disarmed here. (1) Power BI defaults every numeric column to SUM — drag Rank onto a visual and it happily reports "Sum of Rank = 15,051", which is meaningless. Setting Don't summarize turns rank/IDs into labels instead of quantities. (2) The row-level Unemployment_rate column is unweighted — averaging it gives 6.82%, but the true graduate-weighted unemployment is 7.19%. Hiding the raw column forces every report user through your correct measure.

Real-time example: Why the difference matters: Education is a huge category (559,129 grads) with low 4.95% unemployment, while several tiny majors have 15%+ rates. An unweighted average lets 30 small majors outvote 559K graduates; the weighted measure counts people, not majors. Presenting 6.82% to the Dean undercounts real unemployment by nearly half a point — someone with a calculator will catch it in the meeting.

What happens if you skip it: Someone drags the hidden-but-visible raw rate into a card, gets the unweighted number, and now your own dashboard shows two different "unemployment rates" — the exact confusion model view cleanup exists to prevent.

Smart-work tip: Treat rate columns like plutonium: compute them fresh from additive numerators/denominators (Unemployed ÷ (Employed + Unemployed)) with DIVIDE, then hide the raw ingredient. Also note: this dataset has no dates, so no Date Table — professionals don't build scaffolding they don't need.

Stage 4 — DAX Measures (Beginner → Advanced)

Right-click Majors → New measure for each formula below:

// --- BEGINNER MEASURES ---
Majors Count = COUNTROWS ( 'Majors' )
// Format: Whole number -> Check: 173

Total Graduates = SUM ( 'Majors'[Total] )
// Format: Whole number (#,0) -> Check: 6,771,654

Total Men = SUM ( 'Majors'[Men] )
Total Women = SUM ( 'Majors'[Women] )

Avg Median Pay = AVERAGE ( 'Majors'[Median] )
// Format: Currency ($#,0) -> Check: $40,151

Avg P25 Pay = AVERAGE ( 'Majors'[P25th] )
Avg P75 Pay = AVERAGE ( 'Majors'[P75th] )

// --- INTERMEDIATE MEASURES (True Weighted Ratios!) ---
Weighted Unemployment % =
DIVIDE (
    SUM ( 'Majors'[Unemployed] ),
    SUM ( 'Majors'[Employed] ) + SUM ( 'Majors'[Unemployed] ),
    0
)
// Format: Percentage (0.00%) -> Check: 7.19% (NOT the unweighted 6.82%!)

Women Share % = DIVIDE ( [Total Women], [Total Graduates], 0 )
// Format: Percentage (0.0%) -> Check: 57.5% (57.52%)

College Job Match % = DIVIDE ( SUM ( 'Majors'[College_jobs] ), SUM ( 'Majors'[Employed] ), 0 )
// Format: Percentage (0.0%) -> Check what share of working grads hold college-level jobs!

// --- ADVANCED MEASURE (Pay Upside Spread) ---
Interquartile Pay Spread = [Avg P75 Pay] - [Avg P25 Pay]
// Format: Currency ($#,0) -> Shows salary growth potential from 25th to 75th percentile
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Half these measures exist to prevent wrong math, not just produce right math. Avg Median Pay uses AVERAGE because medians can't be summed. Weighted Unemployment % rebuilds the ratio from summed headcounts (Unemployed ÷ (Employed + Unemployed)) so big categories count proportionally. Women Share % divides summed Women by summed Total — automatically weighted by graduate population.

Real-time example: The two-unemployment-values trap again, now in DAX terms: AVERAGE('Majors'[Unemployment_rate]) = 6.82% (each of 173 majors gets one equal vote) versus your measure = 7.19% (each graduate gets one equal vote). The second is the real-world number — and the sign-off checklist literally refuses 6.82%. Same logic for gender: Health is 83.7% women across 463K graduates — a number that only emerges correctly from weighted division, not from averaging 12 per-major shares.

What happens if you skip it: The dashboard ships with plausible-looking but wrong ratios, and because both versions display fine, the error can survive for months until an external researcher compares it to the published census figure.

Smart-work tip: Ask of every ratio measure: "what is one unit here — a major or a person?" Here the client cares about people, so sums go on top and bottom. Keep each measure's check-value (7.19%, 57.5%, $40,151) in a comment so future-you can re-verify in seconds after any change.

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Before comparing any single major or category, you need the national baseline. These 4 cards are that baseline — every later chart is judged against them ("above/below average pay", "safer/riskier than 7.19%"). Benchmarks turn isolated numbers into meaning.

Smart-work tip: Order the cards to tell the market's story left-to-right: how many people (6.77M), what they earn ($40,151), how risky it is (7.19%), who they are (57.5% women). It reads like a headline paragraph without any text.

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The Top N filter is a core Power BI skill: 173 bars need a scrollbar and show nothing; 10 bars show a ranking. And plotting all three percentiles (P25th, Median, P75th) instead of one number teaches the deeper lesson: every salary is a range, not a point. The gap between the small bar and the big bar IS the story.

Real-time example: A top student choosing between majors reads Petroleum Engineering as "median $110K" — good, but flat. The percentile bars reveal: even the 25th-percentile graduate earns $95K (a high floor), and the 75th reaches $125K (a high ceiling). Compare that with a major whose median looks decent but whose spread is tiny: less downside, but also less upside. The advising office can finally answer "what if I'm just average in this field?" — look at the left bar.

What happens if you skip it: Without Top N, the visual drowns in 173 rows; without percentile bars, students fixate on medians and never learn that majors differ in risk and upside, not just level. Careers advice stays one-dimensional.

Smart-work tip: The "By value" box in a Top N filter accepts a MEASURE — that means your Top 10 re-ranks automatically when a slicer (say, a category) is clicked. Try it: filter to Engineering and watch the Top 10 recompute. That is interactivity no static spreadsheet ranking can offer.

Reason: An average describes the center of a distribution; a histogram shows its shape — and shape carries information the average destroys. This visual answers "how common is each salary level across all 173 majors?" — the question behind "what does a typical major pay?"

Real-time example: The average is $40,151 — but the histogram shows WHERE majors actually cluster: a tall pile in the $30K–$40K bands, a thin tail stretching to $70K+. 8. Trainer's Troubleshooting: Common Beginner Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Summing Median, P25th, or P75th: Power BI defaults numeric columns to SUM, which produces nonsense like "$6.95M total median salary" and a meaningless "Sum of Rank = 15,051" (every rank from 1 to 173 added together). Always wrap salary columns in AVERAGE() or MEDIAN()!
Mistake 2 — Deleting Row 23 (Food Science) or replacing blanks with 0: If you replace ShareWomen blank with 0, Food Science looks 100% male when its gender split was simply unreported. Leave unknown demographics as null.
Mistake 3 — Scatter Chart shows only ONE dot: You forgot to put Major_category or Major into the Values well of the Scatter chart! Without a grouping column in Values, Power BI aggregates all 173 rows into one grand-total dot.
