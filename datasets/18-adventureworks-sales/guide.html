Why Do Customer_data and Reseller_data Both Start With Key -1 = [Not Applicable]?
In a Kimball Enterprise Data Warehouse, every fact row MUST have a valid Foreign Key (never null!). When a sale is B2B Wholesale (Reseller), its CustomerKey = -1; when a sale is Direct-to-Consumer (Internet), its ResellerKey = -1! Never delete the -1 row in Power Query — it preserves 100% referential integrity across all 121,253 rows!

4. Phase A — Step-by-Step in Microsoft Excel (Data Model / Power Pivot)

Step A1 — Verify Fiscal Year & Channel Totals

Open datasets/18-adventureworks-sales/data/AdventureWorks-Sales.xlsx. In Sales_data, verify column sums:
• =SUM(Sales_data!O2:O121254) → $109,809,274.20 (Sales Amount)
• =SUM(Sales_data!N2:N121254) → $97,257,907.95 (Total Product Cost)
• Subtract → $12,551,366.25 Gross Profit (11.43%)! You can also load all 7 sheets into Excel's Power Pivot Data Model to practice building the exact same Star Schema inside Excel!
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Enterprise finance has one iron rule: the numbers must reconcile to the source. Before building any model, you prove the pipeline's integrity by hand — summing Sales Amount and Total Product Cost in the raw sheet and computing Gross Profit yourself. This also fixes the DEFINITIONS in your head (Gross Profit = Sales − Cost; Margin = Profit ÷ Sales) that you'll re-encode as DAX measures minutes later.

Real-time example: The CFO's brief makes a specific claim: revenue doubled from FY2018 ($23.86M) to FY2020 ($51.88M) while margin stayed at just 11.43%. Your hand arithmetic — $109,809,274.20 − $97,257,907.95 = $12,551,366.25, and 12.551 ÷ 109.809 = 11.43% — establishes the ground truth those claims rest on. Later, when your Power BI card shows exactly $109,809,274.20, the model is PROVEN; if it showed $109.7M, you'd know instantly something broke (wrong filter, wrong type). That reconciliation instinct is what separates a data analyst from a chart maker — and it's a PL-300 exam favorite.

What happens if you skip it: A model error surfaces in the board meeting instead of in development — and once one number is challenged, EVERY number in the deck is doubted.

Smart-work tip: Keep a tiny "source-of-truth" scratch sheet of 5–6 verified totals per project. Any BI model is accepted or rejected by how it reconciles, not how it looks — make proving it a one-minute job.

5. Phase B — Complete Power BI 7-Table Star Schema & Fiscal DAX

Stage 1 & 2 — Load & Polish the 7 Warehouse Tables

  1. Click Home → Excel workbook → select data/AdventureWorks-Sales.xlsx → check all 7 sheets → Transform Data.
  2. Rename each query to remove _data: Sales, Date, Product, Customer, Reseller, Territory, and Sales Order.
  3. Verify Date[Date] is Data Type = Date, all *Key columns are Whole Number, and Sales Amount / Total Product Cost are Fixed decimal number ($). Click Home → Close & Apply.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: A 7-table warehouse arrives dressed for programmers, not executives: sheet names like Sales_data, cryptic surrogate keys, and money columns that default to floating-point. Renaming to business words, typing money as Fixed decimal, and keeping keys as Whole Number is the hygiene pass that decides whether the model reads like a professional data product or a CSV dump.

Real-time example: The Fixed-decimal choice is the one that bites hardest: 121,253 rows of Sales Amount must reconcile to the cent (your Step A1 total). Floating-point arithmetic introduces tiny drift — sums that show 109,809,274.20000001-style noise and occasionally miss reconciliation by a cent, exactly the kind of defect that erases trust in a finance review. Fixed decimal (the currency type) stores exact decimal values. Meanwhile, the renames pay off in every meeting: "Sales, Product, Reseller, Territory" need no translation for the CFO.

What happens if you skip it: Meetings argue about what Sales_data contains, money totals drift at the 10th decimal place, and joins on mistyped keys silently drop rows.

Smart-work tip: Rename in Power Query (not just Model view) so names propagate everywhere, and adopt the type discipline: Whole Number = keys, Fixed Decimal = money, Date = dates. It's the same typing law every set in this course has enforced — now at warehouse scale.

Stage 3 — Wire the 7-Table Star Schema + Mark Date Table

1. In Model view, verify (or drag) 1-to-Many single-direction relationships from Product[ProductKey], Customer[CustomerKey], Reseller[ResellerKey], Territory[SalesTerritoryKey], and Sales Order[SalesOrderLineKey] (ensure Sales Order filters Sales!) into Sales.
2. Drag Date[DateKey] → Sales[OrderDateKey] (Active), then drag Date[DateKey] → Sales[ShipDateKey] (Inactive) and Sales[DueDateKey] (Inactive).
3. Right-click Date → Mark as date table → choose column Date[Date]! And select Date[Month] → Sort by column → MonthKey!
4. Create a formal Hierarchy on Product: right-click Category → Create hierarchy → add Subcategory, Model, and Product!
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: This stage builds the Kimball star: six dimensions radiating from one fact table, filters flowing one way (dimension → fact). The special problem here is TIME: every fact row carries THREE dates (ordered, shipped, due), but a schema allows only ONE active relationship per pair of tables — so Date must role-play: active via OrderDateKey, and two inactive relationships (ShipDateKey, DueDateKey) that measures can activate on demand. Marking the table as a date table then unlocks all time-intelligence functions, and sorting Month by MonthKey stops alphabetical month chaos.

Real-time example: The CFO's ask "compare revenue when the order was PLACED vs when it left the warehouse" is ONLY possible because of the inactive ship-date relationship — one USERELATIONSHIP measure (Set 12's trick, now on dates) switches the filter path from order-date to ship-date, and Visual 3's backlog gap becomes measurable. The fiscal calendar matters just as much: AdventureWorks' year ends June 30, so FY2020 runs July 2019–June 2020; without "Mark as date table" the YTD functions misbehave, and without the fiscal awareness Stage 4's "6/30" argument the year would wrongly start in January.

What happens if you skip it: Ordered-vs-shipped analysis becomes impossible (one date path only), TOTALYTD/SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR error out or return wrong periods, and month slicers read April, August, December... alphabetically.

Smart-work tip: Hide ALL *Key columns in Report view — a star schema with hidden keys is the industry-standard shape and a guaranteed PL-300 question. And the first calendar question to ask ANY finance client: "when does your fiscal year end?" More BI deployments fail on calendar mismatch than on complex DAX.

Stage 4 — Enterprise Financial & Fiscal Time Intelligence DAX

Total Sales = SUM ( 'Sales'[Sales Amount] )
// Check: $109,809,274.20

Total Cost = SUM ( 'Sales'[Total Product Cost] )
// Check: $97,257,907.95

Gross Profit = [Total Sales] - [Total Cost]
// Check: $12,551,366.25

Gross Margin % = DIVIDE ( [Gross Profit], [Total Sales] )
// Check: 11.43% (Internet = 41.15% vs Reseller = 0.58%!)

// Role-Playing Date Measure (Shipped Revenue vs Ordered Revenue!)
Shipped Sales =
CALCULATE (
    [Total Sales],
    USERELATIONSHIP ( 'Date'[DateKey], 'Sales'[ShipDateKey] )
)

// Fiscal Year-to-Date (AdventureWorks Fiscal Year ends June 30!)
Sales Fiscal YTD =
TOTALYTD ( [Total Sales], 'Date'[Date], "6/30" )

Sales PY =
CALCULATE ( [Total Sales], SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR ( 'Date'[Date] ) )

Sales YoY % =
DIVIDE ( [Total Sales] - [Sales PY], [Sales PY] )

Target Sales (110% of PY) = [Sales PY] * 1.10
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: This block translates finance's vocabulary into DAX: profit and margin as the two base ratios, then THREE time dimensions of the same sales number. TOTALYTD(..., "6/30") hard-codes the fiscal year-end — the third argument tells DAX the year ends June 30, not December 31. USERELATIONSHIP builds Shipped Sales by temporarily activating the inactive ship-date path. And the Target measure derives from prior-year sales (×1.10), so the goal moves automatically as data grows.

Real-time example: Watch the fiscal argument save a board meeting: in December 2019, the CEO asks "how are we tracking for FY2020?" TOTALYTD with "6/30" correctly accumulates July–December 2019 as FY2020's first half; WITHOUT it, DAX restarts the YTD clock in January — reporting a half-year against a full-year target and triggering a false panic (or worse, false comfort). Meanwhile Shipped Sales answers the COO's weekly question "how much revenue actually LEFT the warehouse?" — the ordered-vs-shipped gap is backlog, and it's only visible because one measure switched relationships.

What happens if you skip it: Fiscal periods silently split into calendar halves (Mistake 2), YoY compares wrong months, and the ordered-vs-shipped lens disappears entirely — the model reports only "when clicked" revenue.

Smart-work tip: Build targets as DERIVED measures ([Sales PY] × 1.10), never typed constants — they recompute as data lands. And reuse the pattern trio (YTD, PY, YoY %) on every time-series KPI; it's the same skeleton in every finance BI job you'll ever hold.

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Every executive review opens with the same ritual question: "are we on pace?" The native KPI visual is purpose-built to answer it in one glance — a Value, a Target, and a month-by-month Trend rendered as a background sparkline, with color doing the talking (green = beating 110%-of-prior-year pace, red = behind). It replaces three visuals (a card, a trend line, a target table) with a single executive-readable object.

Real-time example: Select FY2020: Total Sales trends month by month (in correct fiscal order — this is where the MonthKey sort from Stage 3 pays off silently) against the derived target. The Board sees at a glance whether the year is pacing toward $51.88M against prior-year $34.07M — a +52% run that turns the KPI permanently green — and any red month (a soft quarter, a shipment backlog) jumps out immediately for discussion. That's the difference between presenting data and presenting a verdict.

What happens if you skip it: Executives get raw sales numbers with no pace reference, so every meeting re-derives "is that good?" by hand — and the 110% growth ambition that exists in the business never becomes a tracked, color-coded standard.

Smart-work tip: A KPI visual is only as good as its trend axis and target: feed it a properly sorted month field and a MEASURE-based target (never a typed constant), and it stays correct as new months load. Reserve it for THE headline metric — a page of five KPIs is a page of none.

Visual 2, 3 & 4 Channel Margin Paradox Combo + Ordered-vs-Shipped Line + Hierarchy Matrix

1. Line and clustered column chart: X-axis = Sales Order[Channel], Columns = [Total Sales] and [Gross Profit], Line = [Gross Margin %] — exposes the 0.58% Reseller margin vs. 41.15% Internet margin!
2. Matrix: Rows = Product Hierarchy (`Category → Subcategory → Model`), Values = `[Total Sales]`, `[Gross Profit]`, `[Gross Margin %]` (`Bikes = $94.62M / 11.11%`, `Accessories = $1.27M / 49.88%`!).

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: These two visuals are the CFO's paradox made undeniable. The combo chart deliberately puts the absolute (revenue columns) and the ratio (margin line) on one canvas: Reseller's towering $80.45M column sits beneath a nearly-flat 0.58% margin line, while Internet's modest $29.36M rides a 41.15% line. Volume and QUALITY of revenue become one picture. The hierarchy matrix then drills Category → Subcategory → Model to locate WHERE the margin actually lives — the "which slice?" tool that turns a paradox into an action plan.

Real-time example: Read the strategy straight off the visuals: Resellers drive 73.3% of revenue but 3.75% of profit; the Internet channel — 27% of revenue — generates 96% of company profit ($12.08M of $12.55M). The matrix completes it: Bikes are 86.2% of revenue ($94.62M) at just 11.11% margin, while Accessories earn 49.88% on $1.27M. The Board memo writes itself: the reseller channel buys market share at cost; growth capital and marketing belong to the Internet store and the accessories attach business.

What happens if you skip it: Revenue charts and margin charts viewed separately each look healthy — the paradox (73% of revenue producing almost no profit) hides in the arithmetic between two pages, and the Board keeps celebrating the very channel that dilutes earnings.

Smart-work tip: Whenever a RATIO is the insight, always pair it with its ABSOLUTE in a combo chart — a ratio alone exaggerates tiny segments (a 90% margin on $1 is worthless), an absolute alone hides quality. And let the matrix hierarchy answer "where exactly?" instead of building twenty single-level charts.
