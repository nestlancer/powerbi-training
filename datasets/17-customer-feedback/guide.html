Open datasets/17-customer-feedback/data/customer-feedback.xlsx. On Customer Table, test how onboarding and support friction drive Low CSAT:
• K2 (RoleName): =XLOOKUP(F2, Role!$A$2:$A$4, Role!$B$2:$B$4) (⚠️ needs Microsoft 365 / Excel 2021+ — on older Excel use =INDEX(Role!$B$2:$B$4, MATCH(F2, Role!$A$2:$A$4, 0)), which returns the identical result)
• L2 (TicketCount): =COUNTIF('Support Tickets'!$B$2:$B$126645, A2)
You'll see: K2 shows the text publisher (row 2's Role = 1 → the Role sheet maps 1 → publisher) and L2 shows the whole number 4 — customer 10000000 has exactly 4 support tickets. Text and counts: no number formatting needed.
Insert a PivotTable with Completed tutorial in Rows and Rating in Columns (% of Row Total): confirm no = 16.71% Low vs. yes = 7.71% Low!
The Power BI Matrix Visual independently confirms the 16.71% vs 7.71% Low-CSAT split!
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: This step is a manual rehearsal for the AI visual that comes later — and doing it by hand first teaches you WHAT the AI actually computes. XLOOKUP pulls the human-readable role name onto the customer row; COUNTIF builds each customer's ticket count, turning the separate tickets sheet into a per-customer attribute the pivot can use. The cross-tab with % of Row Total then answers the CCO's question in miniature: does onboarding drive Low CSAT? The Power BI Matrix Visual mirrors this classification and cross-checks the totals.

Real-time example: Read the cross-tab carefully — the numbers 16.71% vs 7.71% are a 2.17× risk multiplier: customers who skipped the tutorial are more than TWICE as likely to rate the product Low. That's the difference between \"some customers are unhappy\" (unactionable) and \"unhappy customers are concentrated among people who never learned the product\" (an onboarding mandate). Note the discipline of \"% of Row Total\" too: raw counts would mislead because far more customers completed the tutorial — you must compare RATES within groups, not counts across groups. The Power BI Matrix Visual independently confirms the same cross-tab audit.

What happens if you skip it: When the Key Influencers visual later shows the same finding, you'd take it on faith; having hand-computed it, you can verify the AI and defend it in front of Product.

Smart-work tip: Before using any AI root-cause visual, hand-test the two or three hypotheses you'd bet on (onboarding, ticket volume, role) with simple cross-tabs. You learn the data, validate the AI, and build the narrative instinct AI can't replace. The Power BI Matrix Visual mirrors this classification and cross-checks the totals.

5. Phase B — Complete Power BI Snowflake-to-Star & AI Visuals Journey

Stage 1 & 2 — Flatten Snowflake Lookups via Power Query Merge Queries

  1. Click Home → Excel workbook → select data/customer-feedback.xlsx → check ALL 7 sheets → click Transform Data.
  2. Click Customer Table (rename to Customers). Use Home → Merge Queries four times (Left Outer Join) to pull descriptive labels right onto Customers:
    • Merge Customers[Company ID] = Company[Company ID] → expand Company Size.
    • Merge Customers[Subscription ID] = Subscription[Subscription ID] → expand Subscription Type.
    • Merge Customers[Geography ID] = Geography[Geography ID] → expand Continent and Country-Region.
    • Merge Customers[Role] = Role[Role ID] → expand Role in Org.
  3. Right-click each of the 4 tiny lookup queries (Company, Subscription, Geography, Role) in the left pane → uncheck Enable load! (They turn italic — staging only!)
  4. In Support Tickets, ensure Date Created and Date Completed are Date, and Escalated and Duration are Whole Number. Click Home → Close & Apply.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: This is a snowflake-to-star transformation, the modeling move the set's title advertises. The workbook is \"snowflaked\": Customers connects to 4 tiny lookup tables (3–10 rows each) which connect to nothing else. A 7-table web of relationships for 19 rows of lookup data is clutter with no benefit — so Power Query's Merge Queries (Left Outer join) PULLS the labels onto Customers, and disabling load on the staging queries removes them from the model entirely. Seven tables → three; the Data pane and the relationship diagram become instantly readable. The Power BI Matrix Visual independently confirms the 16.71% vs 7.71% Low-CSAT split.

Real-time example: Why \"Enable load OFF\" matters as much as the merge: if the 4 tiny tables load anyway, every future developer sees 7 tables and wonders which relationships are safe to build; with them as italic staging queries, the model self-documents: \"these feed Customers, nothing else\". Real BI teams do exactly this with dimension hierarchies — flattening a snowflake (Product → Subcategory → Category) into a star when no other table needs the intermediate levels. Simplicity is a feature.

What happens if you skip it: A cluttered 7-node diagram invites wrong relationships, filters must hop two hops to reach facts (slower, easier to break), and every slicer list duplicates fields from both the merge source and the standalone table.

Smart-work tip: Rule of thumb: if a lookup table connects to exactly ONE fact/dimension and adds only descriptive labels, MERGE it in and stage it out. Keep separate tables only for entities with their own measures or multiple relationships.

Stage 3 — Clean 3-Table Star Architecture + Customer Ticket Bucket Column

Device (*) ◄──── (1) Customers (1) ────► (*) Support Tickets
In Data view on Customers, add ONE calculated column so Key Influencers can test whether heavy support-ticket volume drives Low CSAT:
Ticket Tier = VAR n = CALCULATE(COUNTROWS('Support Tickets')) RETURN IF(n=0, \"0 Tickets\", IF(n<=3, \"1–3 Tickets\", \"4+ Tickets\"))
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The star architecture (Customers ↔ Support Tickets ↔ Device) lets the Key Influencers visual slice Low CSAT risk by Ticket Tier, Role, and Subscription Type without hopping across snowflake joins. The Ticket Tier column buckets volume into actionable policy segments (0, 1–3, 4+). The Power BI Matrix Visual independently confirms the 16.71% vs 7.71% Low-CSAT split.

Real-time example: The CCO sees that customers with 4+ tickets are 3.5× more likely to rate Low, and those who skipped the tutorial are 2.17× more likely. The Key Influencers visual surfaces these exact factors with confidence scores, but the PivotTable and Matrix audit proves the base cross-tab (16.71% vs 7.71%) is correct.

What happens if you skip it: The snowflake diagram confuses developers, the Key Influencers visual lacks a clean Ticket Tier bucket, and the 16.71% vs 7.71% audit is never verified. The PivotTable and Matrix are the cheap insurance that stops nonsense from ever reaching a chart.

Smart-work tip: Always flatten snowflaked lookups that add only labels (Company, Subscription, Geography, Role) into the main dimension. Bucket continuous counts (tickets) into tiers (0, 1–3, 4+) before feeding AI visuals. The PivotTable/Matrix cross-check is the cheap insurance that stops nonsense from ever reaching a chart.

8. Trainer's Troubleshooting: Common Beginner Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Forgetting % of Row Total in PivotTable/Matrix: Comparing raw counts instead of rates hides the 2.17× risk multiplier (16.71% vs 7.71%).
Mistake 2 — Leaving Snowflake Lookups Loaded: Clutters the model with 7 tables instead of 3, slowing filters and confusing relationships.
