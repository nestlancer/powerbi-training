4. Phase A — Step-by-Step in Microsoft Excel

Step A1 — Open Semicolon CSV Without Scrambling Columns!

  1. Do NOT double-click bank-full.csv in File Explorer! Because European CSVs use ; instead of ,, double-clicking dumps every row into Column A!
  2. Instead, open Excel → Data → From Text/CSV → select datasets/06-bank-marketing/data/bank-full.csv → in the preview dropdown change Delimiter to Semicolon → verify 17 crisp columns (age through y) → click Load.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: "CSV" means comma-separated, but plenty of real-world exports (especially European ones, where the comma is the decimal separator) use semicolons instead. Excel's double-click importer assumes commas, finds none, and dumps each entire row — 17 fields mashed together — into Column A. The From Text/CSV preview exists precisely so you can catch the delimiter BEFORE the data lands.

Real-time example: A teammate double-clicks the file, sees one giant column of age;job;marital;education;..., and spends an hour with Text-to-Columns trying to un-mangle 45,211 rows — then discovers row-level inconsistencies where quoted fields contained semicolons. The import-route analyst set one dropdown to Semicolon and got 17 clean columns in 10 seconds. Same file, same data, 60× time difference — purely from knowing the trap exists.

What happens if you skip it: Everything lands in Column1 (Mistake 1), no helpers or pivots work, and because the mash LOOKS like text data, the error survives longer than a crash would.

Smart-work tip: In the import preview, always glance at the column count against the data dictionary (here: 17). One second of checking confirms the parse; the row count (45,211) confirms completeness. Two numbers certify the whole import.

Step A2 — Add Helper Columns R & S

  1. Column R — Subscribed (0/1 Flag for Pivot Checks): In R1 type Subscribed, and in R2 type:
    =IF(Q2="yes", 1, 0)
  2. Column S — CallBucket (Dial Fatigue Grouping): In S1 type CallBucket, and in S2 type:
    =IF(M2=1,"1. 1 call",IF(M2=2,"2. 2 calls",IF(M2=3,"3. 3 calls",IF(M2<=5,"4. 4-5 calls","5. 6+ calls"))))
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: A 0/1 flag column is one of the most powerful habits in analytics, because of a magic property: SUM of the flag = count of "yes", and AVERAGE of the flag = the rate itself. One column, two aggregations, no COUNTIF gymnastics. The CallBucket column exists because raw campaign runs from 1 to 63 dials — charting 63 bars hides the pattern, but 5 buckets reveal the fatigue curve the client suspects.

Real-time example: In the next pivot, Average of Subscribed formatted as 0.0% instantly prints each job's conversion rate — student 28.7%, blue-collar 7.3% — with zero formula re-writing. And the buckets turn the marketing question "are we annoying people?" into a chartable staircase: at some number of repeat dials, each extra call starts costing money. Without buckets, a "conversion by campaign" chart would have 63 mostly-empty bars where the interesting 6+ region is a long noisy tail.

What happens if you skip it: You count subscriptions with fragile COUNTIF formulas everywhere, and the fatigue analysis — one of the client's four explicit asks — can't be drawn at all.

Smart-work tip: Whenever a column is yes/no or condition-based, ask "would a 0/1 flag make this easier to aggregate?" — it usually does. And bucket any long-tailed numeric column (dials, tenure, order sizes) into 4–6 named ranges before charting it.

Step A3 — PivotTable Verification

Insert a PivotTable: Rows = job, Values = Count of y (Calls), Sum of Subscribed (Yes count), and Average of Subscribed (formatted as 0.0%). Sort by Average of Subscribed descending: verify student = 28.7%, retired = 22.8%, blue-collar = 7.3%, and Grand Total = 45,211 calls / 5,289 yes / 11.7%! Save as 06-bank-marketing-clean.xlsx.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Putting effort (calls dialed) and yield (conversion rate) side-by-side in one pivot sets up the campaign's central diagnosis: are agent hours going where deposits actually come from? Sorting by rate turns a table of numbers into a ranked target list for next quarter.

Real-time example: Read the sorted pivot as the Head of Retail Deposits would: the bank dialed 9,732 blue-collar numbers — more than any other group — and converted just 7.3%; meanwhile students (938 calls, 28.7%) and retirees (2,264 calls, 22.8%) were barely touched. Rough math says re-routing even 2,000 dials from blue-collar to student/retired segments at those rates wins roughly +430 extra subscriptions without one additional euro of payroll. That's the "smart work" version of campaign management: reallocate, don't just dial more.

What happens if you skip it: The dashboard gets built, but nobody can state WHERE to shift effort — the client asked for priorities, not pictures. The pivot is where the recommendation gets its evidence.

Smart-work tip: Verify with three anchors: the two extremes (student 28.7%, blue-collar 7.3%) and the grand total (11.7%). If all three match in Power BI later, every intermediate segment is almost certainly right too — endpoints + total are a cheap, strong correctness proof.

5. Phase B — Complete Power BI & Charts Journey

Stage 1 & 2 — Load Semicolon CSV & Add Call Buckets in Power Query

  1. Click Home → Text/CSV → select data/bank-full.csv → verify Delimiter = Semicolon → click Transform Data. Rename query to Calls.
  2. Check types: age, balance, day, duration, campaign, pdays, previous = 123 (Whole Number); all others = ABC (Text).
  3. Click Add Column → Conditional Column → Name: CallBucket: campaign = 1 → 1. 1 call · = 2 → 2. 2 calls · = 3 → 3. 3 calls · <= 5 → 4. 4-5 calls · Else → 5. 6+ calls.
  4. Add a Month Sort column (MonthNum: jan=1 … dec=12) if you also chart seasonal calls. Click Home → Close & Apply.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Power Query replicates (and makes refreshable) everything Excel did: the semicolon parse is recorded in the Source step (fixable in one click if wrong), the CallBucket Conditional Column replaces the nested IF, and MonthNum pre-empts alphabetical month sorting. Type checks matter too — campaign as a Whole Number enables the ≤5 bucket logic; as text it would sort "10" before "2".

Real-time example: Note that duration (call seconds) is kept as a number, not formatted away — it becomes the raw material for the funnel stages (≥60s engaged, ≥180s deep pitch). And balance including negative values (overdrawn accounts) is fine as-is: averaging negatives is correct here, unlike the pdays = -1 sentinel where -1 is a CODE, not a measurement. Distinguishing "real negative number" from "negative-as-missing-code" is exactly the kind of judgment a professional makes in this step.

What happens if you skip it: The one-click delimiter fix (Mistake 1) isn't recorded, so next month's export scrambles again; buckets and month sorts vanish on refresh; and chart axes order themselves alphabetically.

Smart-work tip: Before averaging ANY numeric column, scan its minimum value: MIN(pdays) = -1 is the smoke that says "sentinel code ahead — read the dictionary first." Ninety percent of corrupted averages in the wild come from codes hiding inside numeric columns.

Stage 3 — Model View: Create the 4-Row Funnel Stage Table

How do professional Power BI developers build a multi-metric Funnel Chart when stages come from row conditions on one table? With a tiny 4-row disconnected helper table!

Click Data view → New table and paste: 
FunnelStages =
DATATABLE (
    "StageOrder", INTEGER,
    "StageName", STRING,
    {
        { 1, "1. Total Calls Dialed" },
        { 2, "2. Engaged (>= 1 Min)" },
        { 3, "3. Deep Pitch (>= 3 Min)" },
        { 4, "4. Subscribed Deposit" }
    }
)
Select FunnelStages[StageName] → Column tools → Sort by column → StageOrder. Leave FunnelStages unrelated in Model view (your DAX measure connects it dynamically!).
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The funnel's 4 stages are not 4 columns in the data — they're 4 different conditions on one table (all rows, duration ≥ 60s, duration ≥ 180s, y = "yes"). A Funnel visual needs one category field and one value field, so professionals build a tiny disconnected helper table listing the stages, and let a SWITCH measure decide WHAT to calculate for whichever stage is on each row. "Disconnected" is deliberate: a relationship would filter the measure by itself and break it — SELECTEDVALUE is the bridge instead.

Real-time example: Without this pattern, beginners try to force the funnel from four separate measures and get four separate visuals — or they build a calculated table of precomputed stage counts that goes STALE the moment someone clicks a slicer. With the helper-table + SWITCH pattern, click housing = no on your slicer and the funnel instantly re-stages itself for that segment: same 4 labels, new numbers. The visual shape stays fixed; the logic stays live.

What happens if you skip it: No funnel visual (or a static one that ignores every slicer) — and this dashboard's centerpiece dies.

Smart-work tip: The "disconnected parameter-style table + SWITCH/SELECTEDVALUE" pattern is a reusable Power BI design tool — it powers funnels, dynamic axis switching, scenario selectors, and what-if controls. Learn it once here; you'll use it for years. Always Sort by column = StageOrder or the funnel draws itself in alphabetical order!

Stage 4 — DAX Measures (Beginner → Advanced)

Total Calls = COUNTROWS ( 'Calls' )
// Format: #,0 -> Check: 45,211

Subscribed Clients = CALCULATE ( [Total Calls], 'Calls'[y] = "yes" )
// Format: #,0 -> Check: 5,289

Conversion Rate % = DIVIDE ( [Subscribed Clients], [Total Calls], 0 )
// Format: 0.00% -> Check: 11.70%

Target Rate % = 0.15
// Format: 0.0% -> 15.0% management target for the Gauge visual!

Avg Balance EUR = AVERAGE ( 'Calls'[balance] )
// Format: €#,0 -> Check: €1,362

Avg Call Minutes = DIVIDE ( AVERAGE ( 'Calls'[duration] ), 60 )
// Format: 0.0 -> Check: 4.3 mins

// --- ADVANCED FUNNEL SWITCH MEASURE ---
Funnel Volume =
SWITCH (
    SELECTEDVALUE ( 'FunnelStages'[StageOrder] ),
    1, [Total Calls],
    2, CALCULATE ( [Total Calls], 'Calls'[duration] >= 60 ),
    3, CALCULATE ( [Total Calls], 'Calls'[duration] >= 180 ),
    4, [Subscribed Clients]
)
// Respects every slicer (Job, Housing, Prior Outcome)! Unfiltered check: 45,211 -> 40,552 -> 22,674 -> 5,289
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The measures follow two professional rules. First, constants become measures (Target Rate % = 0.15) instead of typed numbers — when management changes the target to 16%, you edit one measure and the Gauge, the matrix's green rule, and any tooltip all update together. Second, the funnel's SWITCH branches wrap each stage in CALCULATE so the stage definitions apply INSIDE whatever filter context the slicers create — which is what makes the funnel live.

Real-time example: Consider Conversion Rate % = DIVIDE([Subscribed Clients], [Total Calls]): on the gauge with no filters it reads 11.70%; click poutcome = success and the SAME measure computes 1,511 ÷ 1,511-context correctly into 64.7%. One formula, every segment's true rate. That's impossible with any pasted or row-level number — and it's why the whole report is built on 7 measures rather than 45,211 computed cells.

What happens if you skip it: Hardcoded 11.7% ignores slicers and quietly lies the first time someone filters; the funnel measure without CALCULATE returns the unfiltered totals in every stage, producing a fake "flat" funnel.

Smart-work tip: Test the funnel measure at BOTH ends after building (hover stage 1 = 45,211 unfiltered; stage 4 = 5,289) and once WITH a slicer active. Three checks prove the SWITCH, the CALCULATE filters, and the slicer wiring all agree.

Reason: A gauge is the right visual when ONE headline KPI must be judged against ONE target — the bank's exact situation (11.7% actual vs 15.0% goal). The needle's position makes "how far off are we?" perceptual instead of arithmetic, and because the value is a live measure, the gauge re-judges every filtered view of the business automatically.

Real-time example: Watch it answer the campaign's strategic question live: unfiltered, the needle sits BELOW the 15% target line — the campaign missed. Set the Max to 0.30 deliberately — a 0–100% gauge would cram both 11.7% and 64.7% into the bottom sixth and the needle drama would be invisible.

What happens if you skip it: The client asked "did we hit target?" — without a target-referencing visual, every answer is a mental comparison against a remembered number, and the miss (or the warm-lead opportunity) doesn't land emotionally.

Smart-work tip: Fix a gauge's max to a business-plausible ceiling (here 30%), not the default; scales are storytelling. And remember the gauge reflects ALL slicers — a gauge per segment via a slicer is often more persuasive than ten gauges on one page.

Visual 2 Outbound Telemarketing Funnel — Funnel Chart

CategoryFunnelStages[StageName]
Values[Funnel Volume]

Click Funnel (inverted pyramid bars icon). In Format visual → Conversion rate, keep conversion labels ON (`100%` at top, `11.7%` at bottom). Hover any stage to see both step-to-step retention (`55.9%` of 1-min calls reach 3 mins; `23.3%` of 3-min calls subscribe!).

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: A sales process is a sequence of shrinking stages, and the funnel is the one visual designed to show where the people leak out. Each bar's size is less interesting than each bar's conversion % from the previous stage — that's where improvement effort should aim, so we turn the conversion labels on deliberately.

Real-time example: Read the leaks like the call-center manager would: 45,211 dialed → 40,552 engaged (90% pick-up: fine) → 22,674 reach 3 minutes (the big leak — more than a third of engaged calls die inside the first minute, a script problem!) → only 23.3% of deep-pitched calls subscribe (a closing problem, and the 11.7% end-to-end number is just these stage rates multiplied). The funnel converts one vague miss ("we hit 11.7%") into two named, fixable leaks. That is diagnostic, not decorative.

What happens if you skip it: With only the KPI cards, the meeting argues about the FINAL number; with the funnel, the meeting discusses the largest leak — which is the only lever that actually moves the end rate.

Smart-work tip: Funnels only make sense when stages are genuinely sequential — pick stage thresholds that mirror your real process. And test with a slicer (try education = tertiary): if stage sizes shift but stage SHAPE stays similar, your script problem is universal; if shape changes, some segments leak differently — target the worst shaper.

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Effort and yield are two different scales (thousands of calls vs 7–29% rates), so they need the combo chart's dual axes — and they MUST be seen together, because the campaign's failure is precisely the mismatch between them. This visual is the evidence for the reallocation decision the client asked for.

Real-time example: The picture argues better than the pivot did: reading left to right (sorted by rate), small student/retired columns stand under a high line, then the trend inverts — the tallest column on the chart (blue-collar, 9,732 dials) sits under the LOWEST point of the line (7.3%). Where the line dips and the columns rise is where payroll evaporates. The Head of Deposits can screenshot this one image as the entire justification for next quarter's target-list change.

What happens if you skip it: A conversion-rate-only chart says "call students" but not "and STOP over-dialing blue-collar"; a calls-only chart says nothing about quality. Neither alone supports the reallocation math — the mismatch is only visible when both scales share one X-axis.

Smart-work tip: This "volume columns + rate line" combo is the standard shape for quality-vs-quantity questions everywhere: campaigns, support tickets per agent, recruiters per hire. Sort by the RATE (the thing you optimize), and the tall-column-low-line cells identify themselves.

Visual 4 & 5 Call Fatigue Column Chart + Lead-Scoring Matrix

1. Clustered column chart: X-axis = Calls[CallBucket], Y-axis = [Conversion Rate %], Tooltips = [Total Calls]. Shows steady decay from 14.6% → 11.2% → 11.2% → 8.6% → 5.8%.
2. Matrix: Rows = Calls[education], Columns = Calls[housing], Values = [Conversion Rate %] & [Subscribed Clients], with Rule-based Conditional Formatting turning cells >= 0.15 bright green!

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The fatigue chart answers "how many dials is too many?" with a decay curve — the client asked at exactly which repeat call payroll starts being wasted. The lead-scoring matrix operationalizes everything else: it's the next quarter's calling list, pre-scored, with cells that beat the 15% target lit green by a rule that updates itself when data refreshes.

Real-time example: The fatigue staircase is damning: call #1 converts at 14.6%, by calls 4–5 it's 8.6%, and at 6+ dials it's 5.8% — agents spending six-plus attempts per prospect are working at 40% of first-call effectiveness, and there are thousands of such rows. Paired with the matrix's green cells (tertiary-educated, no-mortgage clients clearing 15%+), the manager has a complete playbook: cap repeat dials at 3, and feed the recovered hours into green-cell segments — paying for itself in conversion points alone.

What happens if you skip it: The campaign keeps "throwing more dials" at stubborn leads (the classic response to a missed target), which this data proves makes things WORSE — the exact opposite of what the client hoped to hear but exactly what they needed to see.

Smart-work tip: Rule-based conditional formatting (≥ 0.15 → green) beats hand-highlighting because the rule re-evaluates on every refresh and every slicer click — the matrix self-updates into a living target list. Keep volume ([Subscribed Clients]) beside the rate so tiny-but-green cells don't get chased at the expense of big wins.

8. Trainer's Troubleshooting: Common Beginner Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Everything lands in Column1: You left the CSV delimiter on Comma instead of Semicolon. Edit the Source step in Power Query (gear icon) and choose Semicolon.
Mistake 2 — Averaging pdays raw (including -1): Always check data dictionaries for negative sentinel codes (-1, -99, -200) before averaging!
Mistake 3 — Averaging a 0/1 helper column across job groups: Never average job-level percentages; always compute DIVIDE([Subscribed Clients], [Total Calls])!
