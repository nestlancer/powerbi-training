4. Phase A — Step-by-Step in Microsoft Excel

Step A1 — Import CSV & Check Types

Open Excel → Data → From Text/CSV → select datasets/08-chicago-payroll/data/chicago-salaries.csv → Delimiter = Comma → Load. Format columns G (Annual Salary) and H (Hourly Rate) as Currency $#,##0.00.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Before touching anything, you must recognize this file's special shape: it stores two different pay systems in one table. Salaried workers fill Annual Salary (and leave hours/rate blank); hourly workers fill Typical Hours + Hourly Rate (and leave salary blank). Those blanks are structural — they mean "this worker isn't paid that way", not "data is missing". Knowing this BEFORE cleaning stops you from ever "fixing" them.

Real-time example: This is exactly how the intern in the client brief went wrong: they saw 7,361 blank salaries, treated the file as one pay system, summed Annual Salary, and published that Streets & Sanitation costs "$35M" — omitting 1,817 hourly sanitation workers whose real combined cost is $203M. A 6× understatement, caused entirely by misreading structural blanks as empty data. Spotting the SALARY/HOURLY split at import time is the difference between the intern's report and yours.

What happens if you skip it: Currency formats get applied wrong, blanks get mistaken for gaps, and the two-system design — the central analytical challenge of this dataset — is discovered mid-build instead of at first sight.

Smart-work tip: On any import, check which columns can be blank and ask "does blank mean missing, or does it mean not-applicable?" Not-applicable blanks are information — they encode the record's type — and should be preserved, never filled.

Step A2 — Add Helper Columns I & J

  1. Column I — EstAnnualPay (Unified Compensation Column): In I1 type EstAnnualPay, and in I2 type:
    =IF(E2="SALARY", G2, F2*H2*52)
    Plain-English meaning: If the worker is Salaried, use their exact Annual Salary; otherwise multiply Typical Hours × Hourly Rate × 52 weeks! You'll see: I2 shows plain 67608 (row 2 — RIORDAN — is salaried, so the formula just copies his Annual Salary). CSV columns arrive as General format: no $, no thousands comma. Format column I as $#,##0 → $67,608. Hourly rows multiply out — e.g., the first HOURLY worker: 40 × 42.4 × 52 = 88192.
  2. Column J — EmploymentType: =IF(D2="F", "Full-Time", "Part-Time") You'll see: J2 shows the text Full-Time (row 2's F/P code is F).
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: EstAnnualPay creates the one comparable currency the entire report runs on: salaried workers keep their exact figure, hourly workers get annualized (Typical Hours × Hourly Rate × 52). Only after this unification can departments be compared on total cost at all — it converts two incompatible pay columns into one budget-grade number per employee.

Real-time example: Watch the discovery happen: Water Management's Sum of Annual Salary reads a modest $47.4M — until you remember 1,636 of its 2,052 workers (80%) are hourly road crews earning ~$48/hr × 40 hrs × 52 weeks ≈ $100K each. Add the hourly annualization and Water Management's true budget is $238.8M — the #3 department in Chicago, ahead of Aviation. The "$710M hidden payroll" is 22.8% of the workforce that no salary-only report can see.

What happens if you skip it: Every department comparison, treemap, and Top-10 chart reproduces the intern's error — union-heavy departments (Water, Sanitation, Aviation, CDOT) all understate their cost by 50–85%, and the transparency portal publishes wrong numbers to citizens.

Smart-work tip: The ×52 annualization (hours × rate × 52 weeks) is the standard payroll convention; say it out loud in your report ("estimated on 52 scheduled weeks") because an ESTIMATE must be labeled as one. Also keep the two raw measures ([Salaried Payroll], [Est Hourly Payroll]) separate in the dashboard so citizens can see BOTH layers, not just the blended total.

Step A3 — PivotTable Check

Insert a PivotTable: Rows = Department, Values = Count of Name (32,226), Sum of Annual Salary ($2,882,248,082.04), and Sum of EstAnnualPay ($3,592,272,113.64). Verify CHICAGO POLICE DEPARTMENT = $1.44B (#1) and save as 08-chicago-payroll-clean.xlsx.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The pivot pins three canonical totals — headcount (32,226), salaried bill ($2.88B), combined payroll ($3.59B) — that every Power BI visual must reproduce. Note the deliberate choice of Count of Name (a row count) rather than "distinct names": a 32K-person workforce certainly contains duplicate names, and counting distinct names would silently undercount real employees.

Real-time example: Scan the department rows and the transparency story organizes itself: Police $1.44B (40% of combined payroll by itself!), Fire $606M — and then the trap departments where salaried and combined disagree wildly (Water $47M → $239M). Having verified both totals in Excel, when your Power BI donut shows 77.2%/22.8% and your stacked bar reproduces the $3.59B sum, you KNOW the model is intact — including that no hourly row was dropped or zero-filled during load.

What happens if you skip it: If even one hourly row got mis-loaded, [Est Hourly Payroll] drifts from $710.02M and nothing looks broken — the error only surfaces when a journalist compares the portal against the city's published budget.

Smart-work tip: For public-facing numbers, verify with BOTH the column total AND the row count — dollars without a headcount check can't catch a duplicated or dropped row. Count + sum together certify a table.

5. Phase B — Complete Power BI & Charts Journey

Stage 1 & 2 — Clean & Beautify Text in Power Query

  1. Click Home → Text/CSV → select data/chicago-salaries.csv → Transform Data. Rename query to Payroll.
  2. Confirm Typical Hours, Annual Salary, and Hourly Rate are 1.2 (Decimal Number) (blank cells become clean null!).
  3. Optional readability polish: Duplicate Department as DeptClean → Transform → Replace Values (remove "CHICAGO " and "DEPARTMENT OF ") → Format → Capitalize Each Word so bar labels read Police, Fire, Water Management instead of 45-character uppercase strings!
  4. Add Conditional Column ScheduleType: Full or Part-Time = "F" → Full-Time, Else → Part-Time. Click Home → Close & Apply.
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Two jobs in this stage. (1) Preserve the nulls: keeping blanks as null (not 0) is what lets AVERAGE() later skip hourly workers when computing average salary — fill them with 0 and every average in the report breaks. (2) Readable labels: "CHICAGO DEPARTMENT OF WATER MANAGEMENT" is 45 characters; on a chart axis it truncates, wraps, or forces tiny fonts. Stripping the boilerplate and title-casing gives "Water Management" — same data, more pixels for the actual information.

Real-time example: Compare two versions of the Top-10 bar: one with 45-character ALL-CAPS labels squeezed to 7pt gray, one with clean two-word labels at normal size. The second reads faster AND leaves more canvas width for the bars — where the actual data lives. Label polish is not cosmetics; it's chart real-estate engineering. Meanwhile the ScheduleType column turns the cryptic "F"/"P" codes into citizen-friendly words for the transparency portal.

What happens if you skip it: Mistake 1 (nulls → 0) drags average salary from the true $115,916 to a fake $89,439 — a 23% error caused by one Replace-Values click. And every visual fights its own labels for space.

Smart-work tip: Do label beautification on a DUPLICATED column (DeptClean) and keep the original untouched — public portals sometimes need exact official department names for legal text, while charts need short ones. Two columns, two audiences, zero rework.

Stage 3 & 4 — DAX Measures (Handling Salaried + Hourly Safely)

Headcount = COUNTROWS ( 'Payroll' )
// Check: 32,226 (Never use DISTINCTCOUNT(Name) because two workers can share a name!)

Salaried Headcount = CALCULATE ( [Headcount], 'Payroll'[Salary or Hourly] = "SALARY" )
// Check: 24,865

Hourly Headcount = CALCULATE ( [Headcount], 'Payroll'[Salary or Hourly] = "HOURLY" )
// Check: 7,361

Hourly Share % = DIVIDE ( [Hourly Headcount], [Headcount], 0 )
// Format: 0.0% -> Check: 22.8%

Full Time Share % = DIVIDE ( CALCULATE ( [Headcount], 'Payroll'[Full or Part-Time] = "F" ), [Headcount], 0 )
// Format: 0.0% -> Check: 96.8% (31,186 / 32,226)

Salaried Payroll = SUM ( 'Payroll'[Annual Salary] )
// Format: $#,0 -> Check: $2.88B ($2,882,248,082)

Est Hourly Payroll =
SUMX (
    FILTER ( 'Payroll', 'Payroll'[Salary or Hourly] = "HOURLY" ),
    'Payroll'[Typical Hours] * 'Payroll'[Hourly Rate] * 52
)
// Format: $#,0 -> Check: $710.02M ($710,024,032)

Combined Payroll = [Salaried Payroll] + [Est Hourly Payroll]
// Format: $#,0 -> Check: $3.59B ($3,592,272,114)

Avg Base Salary = AVERAGE ( 'Payroll'[Annual Salary] )
// Check: $115,916 (AVERAGEX/AVERAGE automatically ignores null hourly rows!)

Avg Hourly Rate = AVERAGE ( 'Payroll'[Hourly Rate] )
// Check: $48.06
💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: Three DAX lessons live here. (1) COUNTROWS for headcount — every row is a person; DISTINCTCOUNT(Name) collapses "MICHAEL SMITH" the officer and "MICHAEL SMITH" the plumber into one phantom headcount. (2) SUMX for row-wise math: the hourly annualization (Hours × Rate × 52) must be computed PER ROW, then summed — SUM can't multiply columns; SUMX(FILTER(...), expr) walks each hourly row and adds up the products. (3) AVERAGE + nulls: averaging Annual Salary over salaried rows only works because the hourly rows are null, not zero.

Real-time example: Feel the SUMX difference: SUM(Typical Hours) × SUM(Hourly Rate) × 52 would multiply the city's total weekly hours by its total hourly rate — a number meaning nothing. SUMX computes each worker's personal annual estimate first (e.g., 40 hrs × $48.06 × 52 ≈ $99,964), THEN totals: $710.02M. Row-wise-then-aggregate is the fundamental iterator pattern in DAX; every inventory value, revenue-at-price, and annualization calculation you'll ever write uses it.

What happens if you skip it: DISTINCTCOUNT miscounts the city's workforce; the hourly bill collapses to garbage; and the null→0 version of Avg Base Salary ($89,439 vs $115,916) gets quoted in a public portal — Mistakes 1 and 2 verbatim.

Smart-work tip: The FILTER(... Salary or Hourly = "HOURLY") guard inside SUMX is defense-in-depth: even if a salaried row somehow carried stray hours/rate data, it can't contaminate the hourly estimate. Guard your iterators against data you don't trust yet.

Smart-work tip: Color-encode pay type consistently (navy = salary, gold = hourly) across ALL visuals on the page — donut, stacked bar, treemap if possible — so the audience learns one legend. And always anchor Top N filters on the CORRECTED total; filtering on the wrong measure silently ranks the wrong world.

What happens if you skip it: Citizens get a ranked bar list (fine) but no sense of SHARE, and no path from "Police = $1.44B" to "that's mostly officer salaries" — the drill-down detail that makes a transparency portal feel accountable rather than evasive.

Smart-work tip: Use treemaps when categories are numerous (10+) AND a drill hierarchy exists; use bars when you need precise comparison. Turn drill-down mode ON deliberately — without it, clicking Police just cross-filters the page instead of exploring inside it.

Visual 4 & 5 Workforce Donut + Searchable Job Roles Table

Add a 2-slice Donut chart (Salary or Hourly in Legend, [Headcount] in Values) and a Table of Job Titles sorted by [Combined Payroll] with Data Bars on headcount and payroll.

💡 Why do we do this?

Reason: The 2-slice donut states the workforce's structural fact — 77.2% salaried vs 22.8% hourly — the composition that explains WHY salary-only reports understate union departments. The searchable job-title table with data bars is the portal's "show me MY question" endpoint: every aggregate chart eventually raises a specific question ("what do 911 operators earn?") that only a lookup table can answer.

Real-time example: A citizen searches the table for "police officer": the row shows headcount, average salary, and combined cost, with the data bar making its size comparable against every other job title in the city at a glance. A journalist writing about overtime-adjacent pay finds the biggest title-cost blocks sorted for them. This is what "transparency" actually means operationally: not prettier summaries, but a lookup layer under every summary where specific questions get specific answers.

What happens if you skip it: The portal answers only the questions the designer anticipated; every citizen query beyond that becomes a records-request email. And without the donut, the 22.8% hourly workforce that motivates the whole report remains an unexplained footnote.

Smart-work tip: Data bars inside tables let a dense table be SCANNED like a chart (in-cell visualization) without adding a second visual. Sort the table by the money measure, not alphabetically — citizens scan by "biggest cost", and Ctrl+F-style search slicers handle the rest.
